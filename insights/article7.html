The Ladder's Last Rung: When a Maturing Bond Forces the Reinvestment Decision

A ladder works because it makes the decision for you. The last rung is the exception: the money is back, the curve has moved, and this time the choice is yours.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026

The money arrives on a Tuesday. Fifty thousand dollars, the principal of a bond bought five years ago, back in the account because the bond matured. It is the moment a bond ladder is built for, and it is also the moment the ladder stops working. For five years, the ladder made the decisions: each rung matured in turn, each proceeds rolled into a new bond at the long end, no committee, no forecast, no argument. Now the money is back, the curve has moved, and this time the choice is the holder's.

The last rung is the only rung that asks a question. Everything before it was a schedule.

What a ladder is, and why it works

A bond ladder is a portfolio of bonds with staggered maturities — five bonds maturing in one, two, three, four, and five years, in equal amounts. When the one-year bond matures, its proceeds buy a new five-year bond. The ladder rolls forward, the average maturity stays near three years, and the holder is never fully exposed to any single rate. E*TRADE's guide to laddering describes the mechanics plainly: equal rungs, staggered maturities, reinvestment at the long end, and a structure that removes the need to predict rates — because no rung is ever large enough to be a bet.

The virtue is discipline. A laddered holder does not decide whether rates will rise or fall. He decides once, when he builds the ladder, and then the maturities decide for him. The cost is the same as the virtue: when the market changes, the ladder does not. It reinvests at the long end whether the long end is attractive or not.

The curve the money is walking into

That is the problem this November, because the curve the maturing money is walking into is unlike any the ladder was built for. On November 13, 2026, Treasury yields stood at 4.32 percent on the three-month bill, 4.65 percent on the one-year, 4.88 percent on the two-year, 4.98 percent on the five-year, 5.15 percent on the ten-year, and 5.47 percent on the thirty-year, according to Intellectia's market data. The ten-year has traded above 5 percent since mid-September, its highest levels since 2002, and the thirty-year touched 5.73 percent in October, the highest since 2002.

The Federal Reserve held its rate on October 28, 2026, voting 10 to 2 to keep the federal funds target at 3.75 to 4.00 percent, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissenting in favour of a hike. Sixteen of eighteen officials pencilled in at least one more hike before the end of 2026, and the December 8–9 meeting is the last chance to deliver it. The market prices the December hike at roughly five-to-one. The term premium — the extra compensation investors demand for holding long-duration debt — rose about 40 basis points from mid-September to its highest level since 2014, driven by Treasury supply and by private bond issuance to finance AI infrastructure, which the Dallas Fed estimates at roughly $360 billion in 10-year-equivalent duration for 2026.

Credit has moved too. The ICE BofA US Corporate Index showed an effective yield of 5.83 percent on October 28, against 4.67 percent a year earlier — a 116-basis-point rise in the cost of carrying the same credit. Investment-grade spreads widened 10 basis points in October and high-yield spreads 32 basis points. The ladder's maturing rung is not being reinvested into the market of 2021, when the ten-year paid 1.5 percent. It is being reinvested into a market where the risk-free rate is above 5 percent at every maturity that matters.

Three choices, and the cost of each

The first choice is the ladder's own: reinvest at the long end, buy the new five-year bond at 4.98 percent, and keep the machine running. It is the disciplined choice, and in a market paying 5 percent for high-quality duration, it is a defensible one. A five-year Treasury at 4.98 percent is a genuine income stream that did not exist three years ago.

The second choice is to shorten: put the money in a two-year bond at 4.88 percent, or a one-year at 4.65 percent, and wait. The cost is small — ten to thirty basis points against the five-year — and the option is real: if the December hike arrives and the curve reprices higher, the short money can be reinvested at the better rate. The cost is the reinvestment risk itself: if the Fed is done and the curve falls, the short money will be reinvested lower, and the holder will have traded a certain 4.98 percent for a gamble.

The third choice is to spend it. A maturing rung is the one moment in a ladder's life when the money is free without a spread, a commission, or a tax consequence beyond the ordinary. If the holder needs the cash — a tax bill, a roof, a grandchild's tuition — this is the cheapest money he will ever take out of the portfolio. The cost is the income the rung would have produced: $50,000 at 4.98 percent is $2,490 a year, every year, for five years.

One more feature of the maturing rung is worth naming: the principal that comes back is not a taxable event. A bond that matures returns the money the holder lent, and the tax code treats it as a return of capital, not income. The coupon the bond paid along the way was taxed as ordinary income in the year it arrived, and any gain or loss on a sale before maturity would be a capital event — but a bond held to maturity produces no gain, no loss, and no surprise on the tax return. It is the cleanest exit in the market, and the ladder produces one on schedule, every year.

The counterargument: the ladder's virtue is that it decides

There is a strong case for ignoring all of this and reinvesting at the long end, and it is the case the ladder was built to make. The ladder exists because forecasting rates is a losing game, and the moment a holder starts overriding the ladder on the basis of a December meeting, he is market-timing in a trench coat. The 10-year at 5.15 percent is already the market's forecast, discounted by every participant, including the sixteen officials who expect another hike. The holder who shortens because he expects higher rates is betting against a market that has already heard the same news.

But the counterargument has a limit, and it is the one the ladder's own design creates. A ladder is a machine for averaging rates over time, and averaging works when rates move around a stable centre. When the centre itself has moved — when the ten-year has gone from 1.5 percent to 5.15 percent in five years — the average is still catching up, and the maturing rung is the one place where the holder can choose to catch up faster. The ladder removes the decision everywhere except here. Here, the decision is the point.

The implication is a rule for the last rung, and only the last rung. Reinvest at the long end by default — that is what the ladder is for. Override it only for a reason that would survive being written down: the money is needed, the horizon has shortened, or the credit you would be buying at the long end is one you would not choose fresh. A reason that fits in a sentence is a reason. A feeling about December is not. The ladder did its job for five years by removing the question. The last rung asks it once. Answer it deliberately, and then let the machine take over again.
