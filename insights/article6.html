How to Sell a Bond That Has No Buyer: The Odd-Lot Problem

A $25,000 bond is not a small version of a $1 million bond. It is a different product in a different market — and the spread is the price of being small.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026

Three dealers, one bond, three prices $400 apart. The holder owns $25,000 of a corporate bond, investment grade, eight years to maturity, and wants out. The first dealer bids 96. The second bids 97.5. The third does not call back. The bond is not distressed. The issuer has not been downgraded. The holder has simply discovered what every small seller of bonds eventually discovers: a $25,000 bond is not a small version of a $1 million bond. It is a different product in a different market, and the spread is the price of being small.

The odd-lot problem is the bond market's least discussed cost, and for individual holders it is often the largest.

Why small trades cost more

The numbers are stark, and they come from the market's own tape. FINRA's TRACE data, analysed in a 2015 study, shows average bid-ask spreads of 1.04 percent for micro trades under $100,000, 0.28 percent for odd lots between $100,000 and $1 million, 0.13 percent for round lots between $1 million and $5 million, and 0.13 percent for block trades above $5 million. A $25,000 sale pays roughly eight times the spread of a $2 million sale on the same bond, on the same day. On a $25,000 position, a 1.04 percent spread is $260 — before commissions, before any price movement, and before the dealer has done anything except answer the phone.

The reason is inventory. A dealer who buys a $25,000 bond holds an odd lot that is hard to resell, ties up a line on the balance sheet, and may sit for weeks before another buyer appears. A dealer who buys $2 million of the same bond holds a round lot that can be sold on in pieces the same afternoon. The spread is the dealer's charge for warehousing the awkward size, and it is not negotiable in the way a stock commission is. The academic work confirms the pattern: Edwards, Harris, and Piwowar, in a 2007 study in the Journal of Finance, found that transaction costs in corporate bonds fall steadily with trade size, and that the introduction of TRACE price transparency in the early 2000s cut costs across the market — but cut them most for the large trades that were already cheap.

The municipal market is worse. Fidelity's guidance on bond liquidity notes that some municipal bonds can go months without a single trade, and that the last reported trade in an illiquid issue may be stale by weeks. A holder who needs to sell a $25,000 municipal bond is not selling into a market. He is asking a dealer to make one.

Reading the tape before you call

The defence against a wide spread is information, and the information is free. FINRA's TRACE system publishes the price, size, and time of every reported corporate bond trade, and the MSRB's EMMA system does the same for municipals. Before calling a dealer, a seller can look up the last three trades in his own bond: the prices, the sizes, and the dates. A bid at 96 when the last trade printed at 98 is not a market price. It is an opening position, and the seller who knows the tape can say so. The tape also answers the question the dealer will not: whether the bond trades at all. A bond with no reported trade in ninety days is a bond the dealer will price as a favour, and the favour will be expensive.

The four exits

There are four ways out, and they are not equal. The first is the dealer desk: call three dealers, get three bids, take the best. It is the oldest route and still the most common, and its virtue is that it works for any bond, including the ones nobody has traded in months. Its cost is the spread, and the spread is widest here.

The second is the electronic request-for-quote. Platforms such as MarketAxess and Tradeweb let a seller solicit competing bids from multiple dealers at once, and competition compresses the spread even on small sizes. The catch is access: most individual holders reach these platforms only through a broker or an adviser, and the broker's own ticket charge can eat the saving.

The broker's own charges are the hidden line in the calculation. A broker who routes the sale through an electronic platform may add a ticket charge or a markup that absorbs the saving the competition created. The question to ask, before the trade, is what the all-in cost is — spread plus commission plus any markup — and to compare it against the dealer's direct bid on the same basis. A spread of 0.28 percent with a $100 ticket charge beats a spread of 1.04 percent with no ticket charge on a $25,000 trade, and loses to it on a $5,000 trade. Size decides which route is cheaper, and the arithmetic takes a minute.

The third is the issuer. Some issuers run tender offers or buyback programmes, and a holder who waits for one can sell at a published price with no spread at all. The catch is timing: tenders happen on the issuer's schedule, not the holder's, and a holder who needs liquidity this month cannot wait for a tender that may never come.

The fourth is the one nobody wants to hear: hold to maturity. A bond held to maturity pays its coupon and returns principal, whatever the spread would have been, and the odd-lot problem disappears entirely — because there is no sale. The cost is the opportunity: the money stays in a bond the holder no longer wants, at a yield the market no longer offers.

The counterargument: the illiquidity premium

Here is the part the seller's frustration misses. The wide spread is not only a cost. It is also a price — and prices cut both ways. The reason small lots trade wide is that the market pays a premium for liquidity, which means the illiquid bond the holder owns was bought at a discount to a liquid equivalent. A holder who bought an odd lot at a yield 50 basis points above the round-lot yield has been collecting that premium for years. Selling into a thin market gives some of it back, but the years of extra coupon are real money, and they belong to the holder, not the dealer.

The deeper point is that the worst sale is the forced one. A holder who sells a $25,000 bond because he needs the cash this week will take whatever bid is on the screen. A holder who can wait a month, solicit three bids, and let the electronic platforms compete will pay a fraction of that spread. The odd-lot problem is worst when it is combined with urgency, and urgency is the one variable the holder controls.

The implication is a routine, not a trick. Before selling any bond under $100,000, check the TRACE tape for the last reported trades — the price, the size, and the date — so the dealer's bid can be judged against the market rather than against nothing. Get three bids, not one, and let at least one of them come through an electronic quote. Never accept the first bid on a bond that has not traded in a month, because the first bid is the dealer's opening position, not the market. And when the spread is wider than the remaining coupon is worth, consider the fourth exit: hold the bond, clip the coupon, and let maturity do the selling for free.
