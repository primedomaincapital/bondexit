The Tax Bill on the Way Out: What Selling a Bond Actually Costs After Tax

The sale price is not the proceeds. Between ordinary income, capital gains, market discount, and the 3.8 percent surtax, the tax bill on a bond sale can take a quarter of the gain — and the calendar decides how much.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026

The trade is done in December. The bond is sold, the cash is in the account, and the year feels closed. Then February arrives with the 1099, and the number on it is not the number the seller remembers. The sale price was $104,000 on a bond bought for $98,000, and the seller had done the arithmetic in his head: a $6,000 gain, taxed at the capital-gains rate, a little over a thousand dollars. The 1099 tells a different story, because a bond sale is not one taxable event. It is three, and two of them are taxed as ordinary income.

This is the part of the exit nobody prices in advance, and it is the part that changes the answer.

Three buckets, three tax rates

Every dollar from a bond sale falls into one of three buckets, and each bucket is taxed differently. The first is interest: the coupon the bond earned while you held it, including the accrued interest the buyer pays you at settlement. That is ordinary income, taxed at your marginal rate. The second is capital gain: the difference between what you paid and what you sold for, excluding the accrued interest. The third is market discount or premium: the gap between what you paid and the bond's face value, which the tax code treats as its own kind of income.

For 2026, federal ordinary income rates run from 10 percent to 37 percent, with the top bracket starting at $640,600 for a single filer and $768,700 for a married couple filing jointly, according to LegalClarity's 2026 tax guide. Long-term capital gains — on assets held more than a year — are taxed at 0 percent up to $49,450 for a single filer and $98,900 for a joint return, at 15 percent up to $545,500 and $613,700, and at 20 percent above those thresholds. Short-term gains, on assets held a year or less, are taxed as ordinary income. The spread between the buckets is the whole game: a gain taxed at 15 percent costs less than half what the same dollars cost at a 37 percent marginal rate.

SmartAsset's worked example makes the arithmetic concrete. A bondholder who earns $10,000 of interest and realises a $10,000 capital gain in the same year, at a 22 percent marginal rate, owes $2,200 on the interest and $1,500 on the gain — $3,700 in total. The same dollars, taxed the same way, would cost $7,400 if both buckets were ordinary income. The structure of the sale decides the bill as much as the size of it.

The discount trap and the premium election

The bucket most sellers miss is market discount. A bond bought below its face value — a $100,000 bond bought for $96,000 — carries $4,000 of market discount, and when the bond is sold or matures, that discount is taxed as ordinary interest income, not as a capital gain. The only escape is the de minimis rule: if the discount is less than 0.25 percent of face value multiplied by the number of full years to maturity, it is treated as a capital gain instead. On a ten-year bond, that threshold is 2.5 percent of face; on a two-year bond, 0.5 percent. A deep discount is ordinary income wearing a capital gain's clothes.

The mirror image is premium. A bond bought above face value — $103,000 for a $100,000 bond — carries $3,000 of premium, and the holder may elect to amortise that premium against the interest income each year, reducing the taxable interest. Without the election, the premium simply reduces the capital gain or increases the capital loss at sale. Original issue discount is the third variant: a bond issued below face accretes value each year, and that accretion is taxed annually as interest income, whether or not any cash changed hands. The tax code does not wait for the money. It taxes the accrual.

Then there is the surtax. The 3.8 percent net investment income tax applies to interest, dividends, and capital gains above $200,000 for a single filer and $250,000 for a joint return. A seller in the top bracket with a large gain can face 20 percent capital gains plus 3.8 percent — 23.8 percent on the gain — plus ordinary rates on the interest and the discount. The headline rate is never the whole bill.

The loss rules, and the municipal surprise

Losses have their own arithmetic. Capital losses offset capital gains without limit, and up to $3,000 a year of net capital losses can offset ordinary income, with the remainder carried forward indefinitely. The wash-sale rule disallows a loss if the seller buys a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale — a rule written for stocks that applies to bonds too, and one that catches sellers who sell a bond at a loss on Monday and buy the same issuer's nearly identical bond on Friday.

Municipal bonds carry the most common surprise in the market. The interest is exempt from federal income tax, and every holder knows it. The capital gain on the sale is not exempt, and many holders do not know that. A municipal bond bought at $95,000 and sold at $100,000 produces a $5,000 taxable capital gain, and if the bond was bought at a market discount, part of that gain is ordinary income. The exemption covers the coupon. It covers nothing else. Fidelity's guidance on tax-smart bond selling makes the same point from the other direction: the after-tax yield is the only yield that matters, and a taxable bond at a higher coupon can beat a tax-free bond at a lower one once the holder's bracket is applied.

The counterargument: the tax tail and the dog

All of this invites a mistake in the opposite direction. A holder who refuses to sell a deteriorating credit because the gain would be taxed is letting the tax code manage the portfolio, and the tax code is a bad portfolio manager. A bond whose issuer is weakening, whose spread is widening, or whose maturity no longer matches the holder's horizon should be sold when the investment case fails — and the tax bill, even at the top rate, is a fraction of the principal at risk. Paying 23.8 percent on a gain is better than losing 40 percent of principal to a downgrade.

The calendar still matters, and it is the one lever the holder controls without changing the investment case. A sale in January rather than December defers the tax by a full year. A loss harvested in the same year as a gain shelters it dollar for dollar. A bond held thirteen months instead of eleven converts a short-term gain taxed as ordinary income into a long-term gain taxed at 15 percent. None of these moves requires a view on rates. They require a view on the calendar.

The implication is a habit, not a calculation. Before any bond sale, run the three buckets: interest, gain, discount. Check the holding period, check the wash-sale window, check whether the bond was bought at a premium or a discount, and check the 1099-B when it arrives against your own records — brokers report the sale, but they do not always report the basis correctly on a bond bought at a discount or transferred between firms. The exit is not finished when the trade settles. It is finished when the tax return is filed, and the difference between the two is the price of selling without doing the arithmetic first.
